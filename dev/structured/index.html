Fused regression, trend filtering and graphs

These estimators minimize a least-squares loss divided by the number of observations. For a general penalty matrix, the objective is

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y-\beta_0-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|D\beta\|_1.\]

The columns keep their original units and order. Standardizing columns would change which differences the penalty shrinks. An optional intercept is unpenalized and estimated by centering within each training set.

sol = fused_lasso(X, y, 0.02, 0.1; intercept=true)
coef(sol), predict(sol, X), dof(sol), sol.converged
D = graph_difference(size(X, 2), [(1,2), (2,3), (1,3)])
sol = generalized_lasso(X, y, D, 0.1)

fused_lasso adds both λ1*sum(abs, beta) and λ2*sum(abs, diff(beta)). Regression uses accelerated proximal gradient with backtracking, gradient restart and the exact fused-lasso proximal operator of ProximalBase. A signal fit, fused_lasso(y, λ1, λ2), is one proximal step at step size n.

generalized_lasso uses the ADMM split z = D*beta. It factors X'X + rho*D'D; the stacked matrix [X; D] must have full column rank for positive penalty. A rank-deficient X is allowed when the penalty identifies its null space. No ridge penalty is added. Zero penalty returns the minimum-norm least-squares coefficients. Dense designs form dense normal equations, so this solver is intended for moderate coefficient dimensions; sparse signal designs and banded differences retain sparse factors. The default rho accounts for the average column squared norm of the centered design; this keeps the ADMM scale appropriate for regression as well as identity designs. Supply rho explicitly for other scalings of D.

Trend filtering

sol = trend_filter(y, 0.01; order=1)
sol = trend_filter(y, 0.01; order=2, x=locations)
sol = trend_filter(X, y, 0.01; order=1)  # differences of ordered coefficients

Order k penalizes differences of order k+1, giving piecewise polynomials of degree k. Thus order zero gives piecewise constants and order one gives piecewise linear fits. Locations must be strictly increasing; the default is 1:p. difference_matrix(p, k+1; x=locations) exposes the penalty matrix.

For positive order, the specialized ADMM of Ramdas and Tibshirani splits the order-k divided differences, then applies exact TV denoising at each iteration. The signal solver stores sparse banded matrices and vectors, with linear storage in signal length at fixed order. Signal fits do not allocate a dense identity. Orders one through three use a compact banded Cholesky factor and in-place triangular solves; other systems use the general factorization. The default augmented parameter follows the input-spacing rule; rho overrides it on the unnormalized-loss scale. Convergence checks both primal and dual residuals, with the linear-signal rule described below. Always inspect converged when changing tolerances or iteration limits.

Paths and tuning

levels = [0.3, 0.1, 0.03, 0.01]
path = GeneralizedLassoPath(X, y, D, levels)
path = fused_lasso_path(X, y, levels; l1_ratio=0.25)
path = trend_filter_path(y, levels; order=1)
coef(path), dof(path), aic(path), bic(path)

cv = CVGeneralizedLassoPath(X, y, D, levels; nfolds=5)
cv = cv_fused_lasso(X, y, levels; l1_ratio=0.25, nfolds=5)
cv = cv_trend_filter(y, levels; order=1, nfolds=5)
coef(cv; λ=:λmin), cv.λ1se

Levels must be strictly decreasing, finite and nonnegative. These are fits on a supplied grid. They do not calculate all homotopy knots, and coef(path, λ) requires a fitted grid level. ADMM warm starts primal and dual variables and reuses the factorization whenever consecutive levels have the same rho, including the automatically selected value. Fused paths use λ1 = l1_ratio*λ and λ2 = (1-l1_ratio)*λ. Fused paths reuse centered data. Tall dense Float64 designs with at most 2,048 coefficients can also cache the normal matrix, which uses at most 32 MiB. Convergence is checked against the original design; a failed check switches the fit back to direct matrix products.

Double-precision linear signal fits (order=1) use a feasible primal-dual objective-gap check rather than relying only on residual sizes. The gap must be at most 0.3*reltol times the objective, and primal/dual residuals must also be within five times their usual bounds. Checks occur every ten iterations, plus the first and final iteration. This can stop earlier on large signals and require more iterations on strongly penalized cases. Near a zero objective, where a relative gap is numerically uninformative, the original residual test is used. Other signal orders, general designs, Float32 fits and absolute-only tolerances (reltol=0) retain residual stopping. primal_residual and dual_residual always report their actual values at termination.

Default tolerances are calibrated by solver family. For Float64 inputs, generalized/graph lasso uses abstol=1e-9, reltol=1e-8; fused regression and other trend fits use 1e-8, 1e-7. Linear signal fits retain 1e-7, 1e-6 with the gap rule above. Other floating types retain 1e-7, 1e-6. Explicit tolerances override these defaults consistently in fits, paths and CV folds. The stricter residual defaults address accuracy failures on strongly penalized quadratic signals and correlated regression designs.

converged means the applicable stopping test passed. Except for the linear-signal objective certificate, it reports ADMM residual convergence or a proximal-gradient residual, not a certified objective gap. Fused regression checks its residual at the returned coefficients using the original design, including when normal equations are cached. No stopping rule here guarantees a fixed relative coefficient error uniformly over ill-conditioned or nonidentifiable designs. Tighten tolerances and check solution stability when coefficient precision matters; changing df_tol also requires a sufficiently accurate solve.

Degrees of freedom use the generalized-lasso formula rank(X * null(D[inactive, :])), plus one for an intercept, following Tibshirani and Taylor. Inactive rows satisfy abs(D*beta) <= df_tol, with default df_tol=1e-4 in the units of the differences. For signal trend filtering this is the number of knots plus order+1; for graph signals it is the number of fused connected components. With the additional ℓ1 penalty, components pinned to zero contribute no degrees of freedom. Numerical classification near a transition depends on df_tol; a smaller tolerance needs a correspondingly accurate solve. For graph constraints and coordinate pins, regression uses a sparse, orthonormal component basis and calculates its rank after multiplication by the design. A component count alone is insufficient when the design is rank deficient. Paths check the design rank once and reuse a degree-of-freedom result only when the complete inactive-row pattern repeats at the next level. The cache retains one pattern and belongs to that path. General contrasts, numerically ambiguous edge weights and rank calculations retain the dense null-space calculation. These choices do not change df_tol or solver stopping.

AIC and BIC are n*log(RSS/n) + c*df, with c=2 and c=log(n), respectively. They return NaN for nonconverged fits and for df >= n. Cross-validation uses the same grid in every fold and training-only centering. Supply folds for blocked or grouped data. Signal CV keeps the complete location grid in each fold and fits a sparse observation-selection design, so held-out responses do not enter training. Training observations must still identify the penalty's unpenalized polynomial component. CV throws if a fit does not converge. Signal CV uses the generic-design degrees-of-freedom calculation; general contrasts can still require a dense null-space step. The large-signal benchmarks cover direct fits, not CV; they do not establish scalable tuning on million-point location grids.

Comparison with local polynomial regression

Both trend filtering and KernelSmoothing's local polynomial regression estimate a regression function. Trend filtering selects knots through a global difference penalty. Local polynomial regression fits a polynomial in each kernel window, with its scale set by the bandwidth. A jump or a change in slope can lead to different fits, so compare both on the same responses and held-out observations. The structured regression example computes both fits on the same data and reports their errors against a known signal; it does not establish a general accuracy advantage for either method.

Reference conventions and benchmarks

R's genlasso and glmgen::trendfilter use an unnormalized squared loss. Use lambda_R = n*lambda_Julia on identical inputs and difference matrices. The independent golden-data generator and replicated Discovery launcher are in benchmark/structured.
