The lasso and its tuning-free variants

The lasso

lasso(X, y, λ) solves

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n}\,\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

  • The scaling. The loss is divided by $2n$, as in glmnet, so that $\lambda$ is on the scale of the gradient $X_j^\top r / n$ and does not grow with the sample size.
  • The intercept. With intercept = true, $\beta_0$ is not penalized, and at the solution it is $\bar y - \bar x^\top \hat\beta$ for the column means $\bar x$ and the mean $\bar y$. The columns and $y$ are centered on the fly, so $X$ is not copied. Without it, $\beta_0 = 0$.
  • The loadings. By default $\omega_j = 1$. With standardizeX = true, $\omega_j$ is the standard deviation of column $j$, $\sqrt{\sum_i (X_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2 / n}$ (centered only with an intercept), which penalizes the coefficients as if the columns were standardized while keeping them on the scale of the data. lasso(X, y, λ, ω) takes loadings of your own, as the adaptive lasso does; a loading of zero leaves a coefficient unpenalized.
  • The largest useful λ. All coefficients are zero for $\lambda \ge \lambda_{\max} = \max_j |X_j^\top (y - \bar y)| / (n \omega_j)$, with $\bar y = 0$ without an intercept.

The theory of the lasso takes $\lambda$ of the order of $\sigma \sqrt{2 \log(p) / n}$ for noise with standard deviation $\sigma$ and standardized columns. That needs $\sigma$. The variants below do not, and a LassoPath with cross-validation chooses $\lambda$ from the data (see Paths, screening and solver options).

lasso returns a CoordinateDescent.LassoSolution: the coefficients x (a SparseIterate), the intercept, the residuals, $\hat\sigma$ (the standard deviation of the residuals), the passes of coordinate descent and whether it converged. The functions of StatsAPI apply to it: coef (intercept first), predict, residuals, nobs, dof (the number of nonzero coefficients, plus one for the intercept) and deviance.

The square-root lasso

sqrtLasso(X, y, λ) solves the square-root lasso of Belloni, Chernozhukov and Wang (2011),

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|,\]

with the loadings of standardized columns by default (standardizeX = true). The gradient of the loss at the true coefficients is $-X^\top \varepsilon / \|\varepsilon\|$, which does not depend on the noise level, and neither does a good $\lambda$: with Gaussian noise, $\lambda = 1.1\, \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha / (2p))$ keeps every coefficient at zero with probability about $1 - \alpha$ when $\beta = 0$, whatever $\sigma$. The loss is not smooth where the residuals vanish, so each coordinate step minimizes the objective over the coordinate exactly.

The scaled lasso

scaledLasso(X, y, λ, ω) estimates the coefficients and the noise level together, by the scaled lasso of Sun and Zhang (2012):

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta,\,\sigma > 0}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n\sigma}\,\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \frac{\sigma}{2} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

The minimum over $\sigma$ is at $\hat\sigma = \|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| / \sqrt n$, so $\hat\beta$ solves a square-root lasso, with the loss $\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| / \sqrt n$, which one run of coordinate descent solves. $\lambda$ is the penalty for unit noise, such as $\sqrt{2 \log(p) / n}$: the lasso that $\hat\beta$ solves has the penalty level $\lambda \hat\sigma$, which the solution reports in penalty. scaledLasso! also solves it from sufficient statistics, $X^\top X / n$, $X^\top y / n$ and $y^\top y / n$, and from a covariance matrix, one variable on the others, as in the precision matrix estimator of Sun and Zhang (2013).

The feasible lasso

Under heteroscedastic noise, the penalty loadings of Belloni, Chen, Chernozhukov and Hansen (2012) depend on the noise of each observation:

\[\Gamma_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2 \varepsilon_i^2}.\]

feasibleLasso!(x, X, y, λ0) alternates the lasso $\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 / (2n) + \lambda_0 \sum_j \Gamma_j |\beta_j|$ with the update of $\Gamma$ from its residuals, until the relative change of $\Gamma$ is below optTol of IterLassoOptions; initProcedure chooses the initial residuals. The examples use $\lambda_0 = 1.1\, \Phi^{-1}(1 - \gamma / (2p)) / \sqrt n$ with $\gamma = 0.1 / \log n$.

Observation weights and offsets

lasso, sqrtLasso, scaledLasso and LassoPath take observation weights $w_i \ge 0$, with a positive sum, and an offset $o$. The lasso then minimizes

\[\frac{1}{2 \sum_i w_i} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i (y_i - o_i - \beta_0 - x_i^\top \beta)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

Dividing by $\sum_i w_i$ follows glmnet: $\lambda$ means the same with and without weights, unit weights give the unweighted fit, integer weights the fit of replicated rows, and zero weights leave observations out. The intercept, the loadings of standardizeX and $\hat\sigma$ are weighted as well. The square-root lasso takes the weighted norm $\sqrt{\sum_i w_i r_i^2}$ of the residuals $r$, and the scaled lasso estimates $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\sum_i w_i r_i^2 / \sum_i w_i}$.

The data

X can be any matrix and y any vector of real numbers. A Matrix, or a view of a range of its rows or of some of its columns, is read in place. A SparseMatrixCSC stays sparse; coordinate descent reads its columns entry by entry, so for now it is slower than a dense matrix. Any other matrix, such as a transpose, is copied once. The computations are in the floating point type of X and y, so Float32 data give Float32 estimates.
