The lasso and its tuning-free variants

The lasso

lasso(X, y, λ) solves

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n}\,\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

  • The scaling. The loss is divided by $2n$, as in glmnet, so that $\lambda$ is on the scale of the gradient $X_j^\top r / n$ and does not grow with the sample size.
  • The intercept. With intercept = true, $\beta_0$ is not penalized, and at the solution it is $\bar y - \bar x^\top \hat\beta$ for the column means $\bar x$ and the mean $\bar y$. The columns and $y$ are centered on the fly, so $X$ is not copied. Without it, $\beta_0 = 0$.
  • The loadings. By default $\omega_j = 1$. With standardize_x = true, $\omega_j$ is the standard deviation of column $j$, $\sqrt{\sum_i (X_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2 / n}$ (centered only with an intercept), which penalizes the coefficients as if the columns were standardized while keeping them on the scale of the data. lasso(X, y, λ, ω) takes loadings of your own, as the adaptive lasso does; a loading of zero leaves a coefficient unpenalized.
  • The largest useful λ. All coefficients are zero for $\lambda \ge \lambda_{\max} = \max_j |X_j^\top (y - \bar y)| / (n \omega_j)$, with $\bar y = 0$ without an intercept.

The theory of the lasso takes $\lambda$ of the order of $\sigma \sqrt{2 \log(p) / n}$ for noise with standard deviation $\sigma$ and standardized columns. That needs $\sigma$. The variants below do not, and a LassoPath with cross-validation chooses $\lambda$ from the data (see Paths, screening and solver options).

lasso returns a HighDimensionalModels.LassoSolution: the coefficients x (a SparseIterate), the intercept, the residuals, $\hat\sigma$ (the standard deviation of the residuals), the passes of coordinate descent and whether it converged. The functions of StatsAPI apply to it: coef (intercept first), predict, residuals, nobs, dof (the number of nonzero coefficients, plus one for the intercept) and deviance.

Formulas and tables

Loading StatsModels.jl adds methods that take a formula and a table (a DataFrame, a NamedTuple of vectors or any other Tables.jl table) in place of X and y: fit with LassoPath, CVLassoPath, GLMLassoPath and GroupLassoPath, and lasso, sqrt_lasso, adaptive_lasso, glm_lasso, group_lasso and rlasso.

using HighDimensionalModels, StatsModels
m = fit(LassoPath, @formula(y ~ x1 + log(x2) + region), df; nλ = 50)
coef(m, 0.1), coefnames(m), predict(m, newdf)
g = group_lasso(@formula(y ~ x1 + region + x2 & region), df, 0.05)   # a group per term

The formula sets the intercept (y ~ 0 + x1 has none). Categorical variables become dummy columns, coded by contrasts. The group lasso takes the columns of each term as a group. weights and offset may name columns of the table, and rows with missing values in the variables of the model are left out. The result is a FormulaFit, whose functions of StatsAPI forward to the fitted model and whose predict builds the columns of new data by the formula.

The elastic net, MCP and SCAD

lasso and LassoPath take other penalties of ProximalBase in place of the ℓ1 norm, at the level $\lambda$. alpha < 1 gives the elastic net of glmnet,

\[\lambda\Big(\alpha\sum_j |\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha}{2}\|\beta\|^2\Big), \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1,\]

and penalty another penalty whose level $\lambda$ replaces: NormL1(1, w) and NormL1Nonneg(1, w), the lasso and the nonnegative lasso with weights; ElasticNet(1, λ2, w), with the ridge part $\lambda_2$ fixed; Scaled(ElasticNet(α, 1 - α, w), 1), the elastic net with weights on its ℓ1 part; and the nonconvex MCP(1, a) and SCAD(1, a) of ncvreg, whose concavity $a$ is kept. With standardize_x, the penalty applies to the coefficients of the standardized columns, as in glmnet and ncvreg, and the coefficients returned are those of the columns of $X$. A nonconvex penalty has local solutions, and the one found depends on the start: as in ncvreg, lasso follows a path of levels from $\lambda_{\max}$ down to $\lambda$, each warm started from the previous one. The path of the elastic net at a fixed $\alpha$ starts at $\lambda_{\max}$ of the lasso divided by $\alpha$.

The exact coordinate steps of MCP and SCAD need each coordinate problem to be convex, $a v_j > 1$ for MCP and $(a - 1) v_j > 1$ for SCAD, where $v_j$ is the mean square of column $j$ (centered with an intercept, weighted with weights). Standardized columns have $v_j = 1$, so that ncvreg's conditions $a > 1$ and $a > 2$ suffice; without standardize_x, a column with variance at most $1/a$ fails, and the path throws an ArgumentError.

Local linear approximation

lla = LLA(...) computes a nonconvex penalty by local linear approximation (reweighted ℓ1): a sequence of weighted lassos, each convex, with the weights $p_j'(|\beta_j|)/\lambda$ at the previous solution. Besides MCP and SCAD it takes the capped ℓ1 penalty CappedL1(1, a), $\lambda\min(|\beta_j|, a)$ (T. Zhang 2010; the truncated ℓ1 penalty of Shen, Pan and Zhu 2012), and the log penalty LogPenalty(1, ε), $\lambda\log(1 + |\beta_j|/\varepsilon)$, which have no exact path:

  • LLA(), a lasso and one weighted lasso, is the two-step estimator of Fan, Xue and Zou (2014), and needs no condition on $a$ for MCP and SCAD;
  • LLA(; stages = 100) iterates until the weights stop changing, as the multi-stage convex relaxation of Zhang (2010), the difference-of-convex algorithm of Shen, Pan and Zhu (2012, glmtlp's "tlp") and the reweighted ℓ1 of Candès, Wakin and Boyd (2008); CappedL1 and LogPenalty use it when lla is not given;
  • start = :previous takes the first weights of each level from the solution at the previous level, as ncpen does, instead of starting each level from the lasso.

Bounds on the coefficients

lower and upper restrict the coefficients to a box, $l_j \le \beta_j \le u_j$, in lasso, LassoPath, CVLassoPath, adaptive_lasso and glm_lasso, with any of the penalties above. Each is a number or a vector with a bound per coefficient. As for glmnet's lower.limits and upper.limits, zero must be feasible, $l_j \le 0 \le u_j$: lower = 0 gives the nonnegative lasso, and $l_j = u_j = 0$ keeps variable $j$ out. The intercept is not bounded. The penalty becomes ProximalBase's Constrained(g, IndBox(l, u)), which keeps coordinate descent separable: each step clamps the scalar proximal step to $[l_j, u_j]$. A coefficient at zero is then optimal while the part of the gradient that points into the box is at most $\lambda \omega_j$, so $\lambda_{\max}$ and the strong rule use only the directions the bounds allow; for the nonnegative lasso, $\lambda_{\max} = \max_j (X_j^\top y / n)_+ / \omega_j$.

The adaptive lasso of Zou (2006), adaptive_lasso(X, y, λ), minimizes

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_j \frac{|\beta_j|}{|\tilde\beta_j|^\gamma}\]

with loadings from a first estimate $\tilde\beta$: the lasso (init = :lasso, at initλ, by default $\lambda$), ridge regression (init = :ridge) or a vector of coefficients. Large coefficients are penalized less, so that they are less biased, and a coefficient with $\tilde\beta_j = 0$ stays at zero.

Penalized generalized linear models

glm_lasso(X, y, λ, loss) solves

\[\min_{\beta_0, \beta}\; \frac{\sum_i w_i\,\ell(\beta_0 + x_i^\top\beta + o_i, y_i)}{\sum_i w_i} + \lambda\sum_j \omega_j|\beta_j|\]

for a scalar loss $\ell$ of ProximalBase: ProximalBase.Logistic(), the negative log-likelihood of logistic regression ($y_i \in [0, 1]$, usually $\{0, 1\}$), the default; ProximalBase.Poisson(), Poisson regression with the log link ($y_i \ge 0$); ProximalBase.Huber(δ); or a loss of your own. The conventions and defaults are those of glmnet: an unpenalized intercept, the weighted standard deviations of the columns as the loadings $\omega_j$ (standardize_x), and the observation weights $w_i$ and offset $o_i$ of weights and offset. The problem is solved by proximal Newton, ProximalSolvers' ProxNewton, the method of glmnet: each step minimizes the weighted least-squares model of the loss at the current fit by coordinate descent, then backtracks. GLMLassoPath computes the solutions along a decreasing sequence of levels with the strong rule, and stops once the fit explains a fraction devmax = 0.999 of the null deviance, as glmnet does: along a logistic path on separable data the coefficients grow without bound. The solution reports the deviance and the nulldeviance. alpha and penalty give the elastic net, MCP and SCAD, and adaptive_lasso(X, y, λ, loss) the adaptive lasso, as for least squares.

CVGLMLassoPath(X, y, loss) chooses the level by $K$-fold cross-validation, as CVLassoPath does for least squares, with the held-out measures of glmnet's cv.glmnet: the deviance (the default), the mean squared or absolute error of the predicted means, or the misclassification rate for logistic regression. Weights and offsets follow the observations into the folds.

The square-root lasso

sqrt_lasso(X, y, λ) solves the square-root lasso of Belloni, Chernozhukov and Wang (2011),

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|,\]

with the loadings of standardized columns by default (standardize_x = true). The gradient of the loss at the true coefficients is $-X^\top \varepsilon / \|\varepsilon\|$, which does not depend on the noise level, and neither does a good $\lambda$: with Gaussian noise, $\lambda = 1.1\, \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha / (2p))$ keeps every coefficient at zero with probability about $1 - \alpha$ when $\beta = 0$, whatever $\sigma$. The loss is not smooth where the residuals vanish, so each coordinate step minimizes the objective over the coordinate exactly.

The scaled lasso

scaled_lasso(X, y, λ, ω) estimates the coefficients and the noise level together, by the scaled lasso of Sun and Zhang (2012):

\[\underset{\beta_0,\,\beta,\,\sigma > 0}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n\sigma}\,\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \frac{\sigma}{2} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

The minimum over $\sigma$ is at $\hat\sigma = \|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| / \sqrt n$, so $\hat\beta$ solves a square-root lasso, with the loss $\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\| / \sqrt n$, which one run of coordinate descent solves. $\lambda$ is the penalty for unit noise, such as $\sqrt{2 \log(p) / n}$: the lasso that $\hat\beta$ solves has the penalty level $\lambda \hat\sigma$, which the solution reports in penalty. scaled_lasso! also solves it from sufficient statistics, $X^\top X / n$, $X^\top y / n$ and $y^\top y / n$, and from a covariance matrix, one variable on the others, as in the precision matrix estimator of Sun and Zhang (2013).

The feasible lasso

Under heteroscedastic noise, the penalty loadings of Belloni, Chen, Chernozhukov and Hansen (2012) depend on the noise of each observation:

\[\Gamma_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2 \varepsilon_i^2}.\]

feasible_lasso!(x, X, y, λ0) alternates the lasso $\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 / (2n) + \lambda_0 \sum_j \Gamma_j |\beta_j|$ with the update of $\Gamma$ from its residuals, until the relative change of $\Gamma$ is below opt_tol of IterLassoOptions; init_procedure chooses the initial residuals. The examples use $\lambda_0 = 1.1\, \Phi^{-1}(1 - \gamma / (2p)) / \sqrt n$ with $\gamma = 0.1 / \log n$.

Inference after selection

rlasso(X, y) is the lasso with data-driven penalty loadings as R's hdm computes it (Belloni, Chen, Chernozhukov and Hansen, 2012): the level theory_lambda(n, p) $= c\,\Phi^{-1}(1 - \gamma/(2p))/\sqrt{n}$ with loadings initialized from least squares on the five most correlated controls. With post=true, each lasso is followed by least squares on its selected controls; the loadings are updated until the residual standard deviation settles or max_iter is exhausted. The first post-lasso solve uses half the nominal penalty. The defaults are $c=1.1$ with post=true and $c=0.5$ with post=false. The solution keeps the final penalized coefficients, intercept, penalty and loadings in lasso, lasso_intercept, lasso_λ0 and lasso_loadings, separately from the returned coefficients and updated loadings. post_lasso(X, y, sol) refits least squares on selected controls and gives heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors for that regression.

Model and orthogonal score

double_selection(y, d, X; method=:partialling) estimates the prespecified coefficient $\alpha_0$ in original outcome and treatment units:

\[y_i = \mu + \alpha_0 d_i + x_i^\top\beta_0 + \varepsilon_i, \qquad d_i = m_0(x_i) + v_i.\]

An intercept is always included. Choose the treatment before inspecting estimated effects; these intervals do not account for choosing a target by its estimated significance. Write $g_0(x)=\mathbb E[y\mid x]$ and $m_0(x)=\mathbb E[d\mid x]$. The partialling score is

\[\psi_i(\alpha,g,m) = \{d_i-m(x_i)\}\{y_i-g(x_i)-\alpha[d_i-m(x_i)]\}.\]

At the true nuisance functions its expectation is zero, and its first derivatives in either nuisance direction vanish. Its Jacobian in $\alpha$ is $-\mathbb E[v_i^2]$ and its influence function is $v_i\varepsilon_i/\mathbb E[v_i^2]$. These properties explain why accurate nuisance prediction can support inference without exact support recovery.

The asymptotic justification follows the high-dimensional partially linear framework of Belloni, Chernozhukov and Hansen (2014). Working assumptions include iid observations; $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i\mid d_i,x_i]=0$ and $\mathbb E[v_i\mid x_i]=0$; $\mathbb E[v_i^2]$ and $\mathbb E[v_i^2\varepsilon_i^2]$ bounded away from zero; bounded fourth moments of the relevant normalized variables; and sparse eigenvalues bounded away from zero and infinity on the relevant sparse sets. Both nuisance functions must have sparse approximations, with approximation errors small enough for the score remainder to be $o_p(n^{-1/2})$. A representative non-cross-fitted sparsity regime is

\[\frac{s^2\log^2(p\vee n)}{n}\longrightarrow 0.\]

Additional empirical-moment regularity is needed for the particular nuisance fits; the reference states precise conditions. The actual lasso penalties, including loadings, must dominate the nuisance scores with high probability, and optimization error must be negligible for the nuisance rates and the final score expansion. The function does not test these assumptions. In particular, selecting a covariance correction or receiving a convergence flag does not establish valid coverage, and native post=false tuning is not automatically a score-dominating penalty choice.

Post-Lasso partialling-out

Fit the outcome and treatment separately on the controls, in that order. Choose residuals $\tilde u$ and $\tilde v$, center each, and regress $\tilde u$ on an intercept and $\tilde v$. The intercept in the last regression is retained even when nuisance residual means are numerically zero. The refit keyword chooses the residuals while leaving the fitted nuisance records intact:

For non-native OLS refits, the control basis uses the same pinned projection as the shared final stage: center controls; discard intercept aliases whose centered norm is at most $\tau$ times their raw norm; scale remaining columns to unit norm; then use pivoted QR and retain the first $r$ directions, where $r=\#\{j:|R_{jj}|>\tau\}$ and $\tau=\max(10^{-7},\sqrt{\operatorname{eps}(T)})$. This rule is distinct from the solve-based rank inside rlasso and from R's dqrdc2. Each nuisance uses its own selected set, including when the sets differ.

Precomputed fits provide a controlled comparison at a fixed penalty and support:

using HighDimensionalModels, Random

fit_y = rlasso(X, y; post=true, rng=Xoshiro(118))
fit_d = rlasso(X, d; post=true, rng=Xoshiro(119))
ols = double_selection(y, d, X, fit_y, fit_d;
    method=:partialling, refit=true, vcov=:HC1)
penalized = double_selection(y, d, X, fit_y, fit_d;
    method=:partialling, refit=false, vcov=:HC1)

Both results retain the same nuisance_y and nuisance_d objects, including selected, lasso_λ0 and lasso_loadings. Comparing them isolates refitting at the stored penalty. Running native post=false fits is a different comparison: it also changes the default $c$ from 1.1 to 0.5 and the sequence of loading updates. The precomputed interface checks dimensions, element types, intercepts, matching post flags, supports and residual consistency. These are consistency checks; they do not establish provenance. Calls preserve both fits and consume no RNG draws.

Variance and diagnostics

Let $v$ and $w$ be the centered final-stage treatment and outcome residuals, $A=v^\top v$, $\hat\alpha=v^\top w/A$, and $e=w-\hat\alpha v$. The estimated influence values are $\hat\phi_i=nv_i e_i/A$ and final leverages are $h_i=h_{W,i}+v_i^2/A$. For partialling, $h_{W,i}=1/n$ and $k=2$. Thus its HC corrections count only the final intercept and treatment; they do not subtract either nuisance's selected-control count or rank. For the shared double-selection variance formulas, $k=\operatorname{rank}(W)+1$ and $s$ is its nominal selected-control count, including aliases.

vcovEstimated variance of $\hat\alpha$
:homoskedastic$(e^\top e)/(n-k)/A$
:HC0$\sum_i v_i^2e_i^2/A^2=\sum_i\hat\phi_i^2/n^2$
:HC1$\mathrm{HC0}\,n/(n-k)$
:HC2$\sum_i v_i^2e_i^2/(1-h_i)/A^2$
:HC3$\sum_i v_i^2e_i^2/(1-h_i)^2/A^2$
:hdm, partialling (default)Homoskedastic variance with $k=2$
:hdm, double selection$\mathrm{HC0}\,n/(n-s-1)$

The :hdm conventions match hdm 0.3.2, including its classical final-stage variance for partialling. For general heteroskedasticity use HC0–HC3; the classical partialling variance needs an additional homoskedasticity restriction for its usual inferential interpretation. Intervals and two-sided p-values use the normal distribution. confint(result; level=...) can change the interval level.

On a common nuisance support with the same OLS projection, partialling gives the same coefficient, regression residuals and HC0 variance as double selection. This includes well-conditioned common supports; different numerical rank rules can matter on nearly aliased designs. With $r$ centered control directions, the double-selection homoskedastic and HC1 variances are $(n-2)/(n-r-2)$ times the partialling variances. HC2, HC3 and the two :hdm conventions generally differ because they use different leverage or variance corrections.

The result records post, effective refit, vcov_type, selected_y, selected_d, their union selected, nuisance_y and nuisance_d. For partialling, rank=1 and dof_residual=n-2 refer to the final regression. target_variation is $v^\top v/\sum_i(d_i-\bar d)^2$; small values indicate little remaining treatment variation. Passing the numerical threshold does not establish strong identification. max_leverage and influence describe the final regression. show flags nuisance non-convergence without making it fatal; inspect both converged and solver_converged on the saved fits.

Failure behavior

Partialling rejects nonempty forced, although native hdm silently ignores I3 for partialling. Use an intercept throughout; intercept=false, observation weights and offsets are unsupported. With effective refit=true, each nuisance must satisfy $|S|+1<n$ even if selected columns are aliased. Double selection accepts only refit=nothing, since its final OLS is part of that estimator.

Inputs must be finite, with $n\ge3$, $p\ge1$, $0<\texttt{level}<1$, and nonconstant outcome and treatment. The shared final stage requires $k<n$; hdm double selection also requires $n-s-1\ge1$. It rejects $\|v\|\le\tau\|d-\bar d\|$, $\|e\|\le\tau\|y-\bar y\|$ and, for HC2/HC3, $\max_i h_i>1-\tau$. Numerical and option errors raise ArgumentError with the quantity and its value; dimension errors raise DimensionMismatch.

Differences from native hdm

hdm zeroes raw coefficients below the absolute threshold $10^{-6}$, which is not invariant to control units. Julia retains such nonzero coordinates. In test/data/hdm/nearthreshold, scaling a control by $10^6$ puts its nuisance coefficients below that cutoff: native hdm drops it and the effect estimate moves from 0.5 to 0.8. The Julia estimate remains 0.5. The discrepancy is intentional in that test. The default Julia partialling results otherwise retain the native hdm variance convention and nuisance residual path; non-native refit modes and robust vcov choices are explicit alternatives.

R's lm handles aliased columns using its own pivoting tolerance. This package uses the pinned, centered and unit-scaled projection rule described above. The rules can give different numerical ranks near aliases; the pinned rule does not reproduce R's dqrdc2 rank decision.

Double selection

double_selection(y, d, X; method=:doubleselection) implements post-double-selection for the same prespecified $\alpha_0$ in the model above:

  1. Fit rlasso(X, d) and retain its selected controls $S_d$.
  2. Fit rlasso(X, y) and retain $S_y$. This is the reduced-form regression of the outcome on the controls, as in hdm.
  3. Form $S=S_d\cup S_y\cup F$, where $F$ contains the forced indices.
  4. Fit the final OLS regression of $y$ on $[1, d, X_S]$. Its coefficient on $d$ is the treatment-effect estimate.

The implementation computes this last regression by Frisch–Waugh–Lovell projection with the shared pinned basis. For $W=[1,X_S]$, let $v=M_Wd$ and $w=M_Wy$; then $\hat\alpha=v^\top w/(v^\top v)$. The target remains the original coefficient of $d$, rather than a parameter defined by a selected model. Neither $S_y$ nor $S_d$ is evidence that the true support was recovered. The sparsity, moment and penalty conditions above are part of the inferential justification.

Forced controls enter only the final regression, as hdm's I3; they do not alter either nuisance selection step. Pass an AbstractVector with an integer element type, unique indices in 1:size(X,2), and no Bool elements. Bool masks are rejected: pass column indices instead. Unsorted indices are accepted; the result's forced and union selected are sorted. Non-integer element types, Bool masks, out-of-range indices and duplicates raise ArgumentError with the offending value before fitting or consuming RNG draws. Partialling rejects nonempty forced.

Rank-deficient control sets are allowed when treatment variation remains after projection. The pinned rule drops duplicate or intercept-aliased directions from the basis, and rank reports $\operatorname{rank}(W)=r+1$. The final parameter count is $k=r+2$ and dof_residual is $n-k$. If the treatment lies in the control span, including when it is a linear combination of forced controls, the residualized-target guard raises ArgumentError. The numerical rules in the failure section also apply near collinearity and saturation.

The default vcov=:hdm uses

\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{hdm}}(\hat\alpha) =\mathrm{HC0}\,\frac{n}{n-s-1},\qquad s=|S|.\]

The nominal $s$ counts forced controls and aliases, even when they add no rank. With full-rank controls, $k=s+2$: the hdm denominator counts one parameter fewer than HC1's $n-k=n-s-2$. This is a distinct convention; vcov=:HC1 uses the rank-based parameter count, as do the other rank-based variance formulas in the table. In particular, an aliased set can satisfy $k<n$ while violating hdm's additional requirement $n-s-1\ge1$. The final OLS is part of the estimator for either post flag, so double selection accepts only refit=nothing.

Precomputed fits allow a matched comparison across the two methods and several variance choices, without changing nuisance tuning or making new random draws:

using HighDimensionalModels, Random

rng = Xoshiro(119)
fit_d = rlasso(X, d; post=true, rng)
fit_y = rlasso(X, y; post=true, rng)
effects = Dict()
for method in (:doubleselection, :partialling), covariance in (:hdm, :HC0, :HC1, :HC3)
    effects[(method, covariance)] = double_selection(y, d, X, fit_y, fit_d;
        method, vcov=covariance)
end

Every result retains the same nuisance objects, supports, penalties and loadings. The final layouts and their variance conventions remain method-specific. Inspect selected_y, selected_d, forced and selected to see how the union was formed, and the saved fits' tuning, converged and solver_converged fields to assess the nuisance computations. These are diagnostics, not evidence that the inferential assumptions hold.

Observation weights and offsets

lasso, sqrt_lasso, scaled_lasso and LassoPath take observation weights $w_i \ge 0$, with a positive sum, and an offset $o$. The lasso then minimizes

\[\frac{1}{2 \sum_i w_i} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i (y_i - o_i - \beta_0 - x_i^\top \beta)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j |\beta_j|.\]

Dividing by $\sum_i w_i$ follows glmnet: $\lambda$ means the same with and without weights, unit weights give the unweighted fit, integer weights the fit of replicated rows, and zero weights leave observations out. The intercept, the loadings of standardize_x and $\hat\sigma$ are weighted as well. The square-root lasso takes the weighted norm $\sqrt{\sum_i w_i r_i^2}$ of the residuals $r$, and the scaled lasso estimates $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\sum_i w_i r_i^2 / \sum_i w_i}$.

The data

X can be any matrix and y any vector of real numbers. A Matrix, or a view of a range of its rows or of some of its columns, is read in place. A SparseMatrixCSC stays sparse; coordinate descent reads its columns entry by entry, so for now it is slower than a dense matrix. Any other matrix, such as a transpose, is copied once. The computations are in the floating point type of X and y, so Float32 data give Float32 estimates.
