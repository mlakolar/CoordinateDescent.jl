The group lasso

The group lasso of Yuan and Lin (2006) penalizes groups of coefficients together,

\[\underset{\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n}\,\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^K w_k \|\beta_{G_k}\|_2,\]

for groups $G_1, \dots, G_K$ that partition the coefficients, so that a group is zero or nonzero as a whole.

Coordinate descent works on an AtomIterate of ProximalBase, whose coordinates are the groups, with the penalty GroupNormL2(λ, groups), whose weights are $w_k = \sqrt{|G_k|}$ by default and w in GroupNormL2(λ, groups, w). A weight of zero leaves a group unpenalized. Each step updates one group at once, with the largest eigenvalue of $X_G^\top X_G / n$ as the curvature (Yang and Zou, 2015). CDLeastSquaresLoss, also with an intercept, and CDQuadraticLoss support groups.

using HighDimensionalModels, ProximalBase, LinearAlgebra, Random

rng = Xoshiro(1)
n, p = 200, 50
X = randn(rng, n, p)
y = X[:, 1:5] * ones(5) + randn(rng, n)

groups = [1:5, 6:10, 11:20, 21:50]
g = GroupNormL2(0.1, groups)         # weights √|Gₖ|
x = AtomIterate(p, groups)
coordinate_descent!(x, CDLeastSquaresLoss(y, X), g)
[norm(x[G]) for G in groups]         # the first group is selected
4-element Vector{Float64}:

 0.0
 0.0

All groups are zero for $\lambda \ge \max_k \|X_{G_k}^\top y\| / (n w_k)$. With warm_start = false, coordinate_descent! solves along a path of penalty levels from there, and screens the groups with the strong rule. The example on the group lasso chooses $\lambda$ on a validation set and checks the optimality conditions.

The estimators

group_lasso(X, y, λ, groups) and GroupLassoPath(X, y, groups) are the group lasso with an unpenalized intercept and, by default (standardize = :orthonormal), the penalty on the fitted values of each group,

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_k w_k\,\frac{\|\tilde X_{G_k}\beta_{G_k}\|_2}{\sqrt{n}},\]

with $\tilde X_G$ the centered columns of group $G$ and $w_k = \sqrt{|G_k|}$ by default. This makes the estimate invariant to a change of basis within a group (Simon and Tibshirani, 2012), as in R's grpreg. It is fitted on orthonormalized groups: each $\tilde X_G$ is factored by a pivoted QR as $Z_G\tilde R$ with $Z_G^\top Z_G/n = I$, the group lasso is solved in $\theta_G = \tilde R\beta_G$ by block coordinate descent, whose steps are then exact, and the coefficients are mapped back; columns of a group that are combinations of its other columns get zero coefficients. With standardize = :none, the penalty is $\lambda\sum_k w_k\|\beta_{G_k}\|_2$ on the columns as given. The groups must be disjoint and cover the columns; a column alone is a group of its own, and a zero weight leaves a group unpenalized. The solutions report the active groups.

sparse_group_lasso(X, y, λ, groups) and SparseGroupLassoPath(X, y, groups) are the sparse group lasso of Simon, Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2013),

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\Big(\alpha\sum_j |\tilde\beta_j| + (1 - \alpha)\sum_k w_k\|\tilde\beta_{G_k}\|_2\Big),\]

which selects groups and, within them, variables. With standardize_x (the default), $\tilde\beta = s\beta$ are the coefficients of the standardized columns, as in R's SGL, whose default alpha = 0.95 it shares; alpha = 1 is the lasso and alpha = 0 the group lasso on the columns as given. The groups are not orthonormalized, since the ℓ1 term is not invariant to a change of basis within a group. Paths screen groups by the level at which each would be zero: a group at zero is optimal while $\lVert\mathrm{soft}(\nabla_{G_k}, \lambda\alpha)\rVert_2 \le \lambda(1 - \alpha) w_k$.

The multi-response lasso

With several responses that share the predictors, multi_lasso(X, Y, λ) and MultiLassoPath(X, Y) fit

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|Y - 1\beta_0^\top - XB\|_F^2 + \lambda\sum_k \omega_k\|B_{k\cdot}\|_2,\]

the group lasso on the rows of the $p \times q$ coefficient matrix $B$, which selects the same predictors for every response (glmnet's mgaussian). The curvature of the loss over a row is a multiple of the identity, so each block step on a row is exact. The solutions report $B$ as a sparse matrix, the intercepts of the responses and the active rows.

Task-specific designs

When the responses do not share the observations, multitask_lasso(Xs, ys, λ) and MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) fit $q$ regressions $y_t = \beta_{0t} + X_t b_t + e_t$, each with its own $n_t \times p$ design Xs[t] and response ys[t]. The designs have the same $p$ predictors in the same order, and the sample sizes $n_t$ may differ. With $B = [b_1 \cdots b_q]$ and $b_{tj}$ the coefficient of predictor $j$ in task $t$, they minimize

\[\sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t}{2n_t}\,\|y_t - \beta_{0t} - X_t b_t\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j\,\big\|(s_{1j}b_{1j}, \dots, s_{qj}b_{qj})\big\|_2,\]

the row penalty of the multi-response lasso, which selects a predictor for all the tasks or for none while its coefficients differ across tasks (Obozinski, Taskar and Jordan, 2010). The coefficients are returned on the original scale.

  • Task weights. task_weights = :equal (the default) takes $c_t = 1$, so that the mean squared error of each task counts once, whatever its size. task_weights = :observations takes $c_t = q n_t / N$ with $N = \sum_t n_t$, so that each observation counts once: the loss is $q$ times the pooled loss $\sum_t \|y_t - \beta_{0t} - X_t b_t\|^2 / (2N)$, scaled so that equal sizes give $c_t = 1$. The two coincide when the tasks have the same size; otherwise :observations gives the larger tasks more weight. A vector of positive weights sets the $c_t$ directly.
  • Standardization. With standardize_x = true (the default), $s_{tj}$ is the standard deviation of column $j$ within task $t$ (with divisor $n_t$, about the column mean with an intercept and about zero otherwise), and the penalty acts on the coefficients $s_{tj} b_{tj}$ of the columns standardized within each task. A predictor is then penalized on the same scale in every task: rescaling its column in one task rescales its coefficient in that task and changes nothing else. With standardize_x = false, $s_{tj} = 1$, and the units of each task's column enter the norm of the row. A column that is zero within a task, or constant within it with an intercept, gets a zero coefficient in that task and is left out of the fit; without an intercept, a nonzero constant column is an ordinary predictor.
  • Penalty factors. penalty_factors are the $\omega_j$, ones by default, finite and nonnegative, with at least one positive. A zero factor leaves a predictor unpenalized. It is allowed with levels of your own only: no level makes the coefficients of an unpenalized predictor zero, so the automatic grid below, and hence cross-validation, needs positive factors.
  • Intercepts. Each task has an unpenalized intercept $\beta_{0t} = \bar y_t - \bar x_t^\top b_t$ (intercept = true, the default); with intercept = false they are zero and nothing is centered.
  • Reductions. With one task, the problem is lasso(X, y, λ) with the same intercept and standardize_x (whose defaults in lasso are false), and with a weight $c$ it is the lasso at $\lambda / c$. When every task has the same design $X$ and all the weights are $c$, $s_{tj} = s_j$ and, with the default penalty factors, the problem is multi_lasso(X, Y, λ / c) with $Y = [y_1 \cdots y_q]$; a dense shared design is fitted with the model of multi_lasso. Its nobs is then $qn$, where multi_lasso reports $n$.
  • Method. The tasks are centered and standardized each on its own, the rows of task $t$ are scaled by $\sqrt{N c_t / n_t}$, and the blocks are placed on the diagonal of a sparse $N \times pq$ design over $\operatorname{vec}(B)$, whose groups are the rows of $B$; group coordinate descent solves the least-squares problem on it, with coordinate orders drawn from rng. The columns of different tasks are orthogonal, so the curvature over a row is diagonal. With standardization and equal weights it is a multiple of the identity and each block step is exact; otherwise a step minimizes a majorization, and convergence takes more passes.
  • Sparse designs. A sparse $X_t$ stays sparse without an intercept. With an intercept, centering fills it in: its block in the stacked design is dense, about $n_t p$ stored entries.
  • The results. A MultitaskLassoSolution holds $B$ as a sparse $p \times q$ matrix, the intercepts, the active rows, nobs ($N$), task_nobs (the $n_t$) and the task weights. coef gives the $(p + 1) \times q$ matrix with the intercepts in the first row, predict(sol, Xs_new) a vector with the predictions of each task at its own new design, and dof the number of nonzero coefficients plus $q$ with an intercept.

MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) solves along a decreasing sequence of levels, each warm started from the previous solution, with the sequential strong rule on the rows as in MultiLassoPath. By default it has nλ = 100 levels spaced evenly on the log scale from $\lambda_{\max}(1 + \sqrt{\varepsilon})$, where

\[\lambda_{\max} = \max_j \frac{1}{\omega_j}\,\Big\|\Big(\frac{c_t\,\tilde x_{tj}^\top \tilde y_t}{n_t\,s_{tj}}\Big)_{t:\,s_{tj} > 0}\Big\|_2\]

is the smallest level whose solution is $B = 0$ ($\tilde x_{tj}$ and $\tilde y_t$ are centered with an intercept), down to λminratio times $\lambda_{\max}$, with λminratio 0.01 when $N < pq$ and $10^{-4}$ otherwise: the grid of LassoPath for one task and of MultiLassoPath for a shared design. MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys, λpath) takes levels of your own, and max_rows stops the path after the first level with more nonzero rows. coef(path) gives a matrix per level, coef(path, λ) one matrix, interpolated in $\lambda$, and predict(path, Xs_new) a vector of $q$ matrices, of size $n_t \times L$ for $L$ levels. fit(MultitaskLassoPath, Xs, ys) and fit(CVMultitaskLassoPath, Xs, ys) are the constructors.

CVMultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) chooses the level by $K$-fold cross-validation (nfolds = 10), with the folds drawn within each task: the observations of each task are assigned to the $K$ folds in a balanced way, as CVLassoPath assigns those of one data set, so that every fold holds out part of every task. folds takes one vector of labels $1, \dots, K$ per task instead, with every label used in every task. The path on all the data gives the levels, and each training fold refits the same procedure on its own observations: the centering, the scales, the :observations weights and the stacked design are computed again, so nothing leaks from the held-out part. Every training fold must keep at least two observations of each task with an intercept, and one without. The held-out error of fold $k$ at a level is

\[e_k = \frac{1}{w_k}\sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t}{n_t}\sum_{i \in \mathrm{test}(t, k)} (y_{ti} - \hat y_{ti})^2, \qquad w_k = \sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t\, m_{tk}}{n_t},\]

with $m_{tk}$ the number of observations of task $t$ in fold $k$, and $c_t$, $n_t$ those of the full data. cvm averages the $e_k$ with the weights $w_k$, and cvse, λmin and λ1se follow as for CVLassoPath. A fold path that stops early leaves the remaining levels out of the averages, and the levels that no fold reaches have NaN in cvm, cvse and cvm_task and are not candidates for λmin and λ1se. At the levels every fold reaches, cvm is $\sum_t c_t \mathrm{MSE}_t / \sum_t c_t$, the held-out counterpart of the training loss, where $\mathrm{MSE}_t$, the held-out mean squared error of task $t$, is cvm_task[t, :]. With one task and the same folds, cvm, cvse, λmin and λ1se are those of CVLassoPath(X, y; intercept = true). nrows counts the nonzero rows of path at each level, and coef(cv) and predict(cv, Xs_new) use the solution at λ1se, or at λmin with λ = :λmin. The folds run in parallel on the available threads, each with a generator seeded from rng, and the result does not depend on the number of threads. The example on multitask regression fits three tasks of different sizes and compares the selection with separate lasso fits.
