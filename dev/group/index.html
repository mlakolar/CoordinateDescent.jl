The group lasso

The group lasso of Yuan and Lin (2006) penalizes groups of coefficients together,

\[\underset{\beta}{\text{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{2n}\,\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^K w_k \|\beta_{G_k}\|_2,\]

for groups $G_1, \dots, G_K$ that partition the coefficients, so that a group is zero or nonzero as a whole.

Coordinate descent works on an AtomIterate of ProximalBase, whose coordinates are the groups, with the penalty GroupNormL2(λ, groups), whose weights are $w_k = \sqrt{|G_k|}$ by default and w in GroupNormL2(λ, groups, w). A weight of zero leaves a group unpenalized. Each step updates one group at once, with the largest eigenvalue of $X_G^\top X_G / n$ as the curvature (Yang and Zou, 2015). CDLeastSquaresLoss, also with an intercept, and CDQuadraticLoss support groups.

using HighDimensionalModels, ProximalBase, LinearAlgebra, Random

rng = Xoshiro(1)
n, p = 200, 50
X = randn(rng, n, p)
y = X[:, 1:5] * ones(5) + randn(rng, n)

groups = [1:5, 6:10, 11:20, 21:50]
g = GroupNormL2(0.1, groups)         # weights √|Gₖ|
x = AtomIterate(p, groups)
coordinate_descent!(x, CDLeastSquaresLoss(y, X), g)
[norm(x[G]) for G in groups]         # the first group is selected
4-element Vector{Float64}:
 2.110219582809482
 0.0
 0.0
 0.0

All groups are zero for $\lambda \ge \max_k \|X_{G_k}^\top y\| / (n w_k)$. With warm_start = false, coordinate_descent! solves along a path of penalty levels from there, and screens the groups with the strong rule. The example on the group lasso chooses $\lambda$ on a validation set and checks the optimality conditions.

The estimators

group_lasso(X, y, λ, groups) and GroupLassoPath(X, y, groups) are the group lasso with an unpenalized intercept and, by default (standardize = :orthonormal), the penalty on the fitted values of each group,

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_k w_k\,\frac{\|\tilde X_{G_k}\beta_{G_k}\|_2}{\sqrt{n}},\]

with $\tilde X_G$ the centered columns of group $G$ and $w_k = \sqrt{|G_k|}$ by default. This makes the estimate invariant to a change of basis within a group (Simon and Tibshirani, 2012), as in R's grpreg. It is fitted on orthonormalized groups: each $\tilde X_G$ is factored by a pivoted QR as $Z_G\tilde R$ with $Z_G^\top Z_G/n = I$, the group lasso is solved in $\theta_G = \tilde R\beta_G$ by block coordinate descent, whose steps are then exact, and the coefficients are mapped back; columns of a group that are combinations of its other columns get zero coefficients. With standardize = :none, the penalty is $\lambda\sum_k w_k\|\beta_{G_k}\|_2$ on the columns as given. The groups must be disjoint and cover the columns; a column alone is a group of its own, and a zero weight leaves a group unpenalized. The solutions report the active groups.

sparse_group_lasso(X, y, λ, groups) and SparseGroupLassoPath(X, y, groups) are the sparse group lasso of Simon, Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2013),

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0 - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\Big(\alpha\sum_j |\tilde\beta_j| + (1 - \alpha)\sum_k w_k\|\tilde\beta_{G_k}\|_2\Big),\]

which selects groups and, within them, variables. With standardize_x (the default), $\tilde\beta = s\beta$ are the coefficients of the standardized columns, as in R's SGL, whose default alpha = 0.95 it shares; alpha = 1 is the lasso and alpha = 0 the group lasso on the columns as given. The groups are not orthonormalized, since the ℓ1 term is not invariant to a change of basis within a group. Paths screen groups by the level at which each would be zero: a group at zero is optimal while $\lVert\mathrm{soft}(\nabla_{G_k}, \lambda\alpha)\rVert_2 \le \lambda(1 - \alpha) w_k$.

The multi-response lasso

With several responses that share the predictors, multi_lasso(X, Y, λ) and MultiLassoPath(X, Y) fit

\[\frac{1}{2n}\|Y - 1\beta_0^\top - XB\|_F^2 + \lambda\sum_k \omega_k\|B_{k\cdot}\|_2,\]

the group lasso on the rows of the $p \times q$ coefficient matrix $B$, which selects rows across responses (glmnet's mgaussian). Entries within a selected row can still be zero. The curvature of the loss over a row is a multiple of the identity, so each block step on a row is exact. The solutions report $B$ as a sparse matrix, the intercepts of the responses and the active rows.

Task-specific designs

When the responses do not share the observations, multitask_lasso(Xs, ys, λ) and MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) fit $q$ regressions $y_t = \beta_{0t} + X_t b_t + e_t$, each with its own $n_t \times p$ design Xs[t] and response ys[t]. The designs have the same $p$ predictors in the same order, and the sample sizes $n_t$ may differ. With $B = [b_1 \cdots b_q]$ and $b_{tj}$ the coefficient of predictor $j$ in task $t$, they minimize

\[\sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t}{2n_t}\,\|y_t - \beta_{0t} - X_t b_t\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^p \omega_j\,\big\|(s_{1j}b_{1j}, \dots, s_{qj}b_{qj})\big\|_2,\]

the row penalty of the multi-response lasso, which selects predictors across tasks while allowing zero entries within selected rows (Obozinski, Taskar and Jordan, 2010). The coefficients are returned on the original scale.

  • Task weights. task_weights = :equal (the default) takes $c_t = 1$, so that the mean squared error of each task counts once, whatever its size. task_weights = :observations takes $c_t = q n_t / N$ with $N = \sum_t n_t$, so that each observation counts once: the loss is $q$ times the pooled loss $\sum_t \|y_t - \beta_{0t} - X_t b_t\|^2 / (2N)$, scaled so that equal sizes give $c_t = 1$. The two coincide when the tasks have the same size; otherwise :observations gives the larger tasks more weight. A vector of positive weights sets the $c_t$ directly.
  • Standardization. With standardize_x = true (the default), $s_{tj}$ is the standard deviation of column $j$ within task $t$ (with divisor $n_t$, about the column mean with an intercept and about zero otherwise), and the penalty acts on the coefficients $s_{tj} b_{tj}$ of the columns standardized within each task. A predictor is then penalized on the same scale in every task: rescaling its column in one task rescales its coefficient in that task and changes nothing else. With standardize_x = false, $s_{tj} = 1$, and the units of each task's column enter the norm of the row. A column that is zero within a task, or constant within it with an intercept, gets a zero coefficient in that task and is left out of the fit; without an intercept, a nonzero constant column is an ordinary predictor.
  • Penalty factors. penalty_factors are the $\omega_j$, ones by default, finite and nonnegative, with at least one positive. A zero factor leaves a predictor unpenalized. It is allowed with levels of your own only: no level makes the coefficients of an unpenalized predictor zero, so the automatic grid below, and hence cross-validation, needs positive factors.
  • Intercepts. Each task has an unpenalized intercept $\beta_{0t} = \bar y_t - \bar x_t^\top b_t$ (intercept = true, the default); with intercept = false they are zero and nothing is centered.
  • Reductions. With one task, the problem is lasso(X, y, λ) with the same intercept and standardize_x (whose defaults in lasso are false), and with a weight $c$ it is the lasso at $\lambda / c$. When every task has the same design $X$ and all the weights are $c$, $s_{tj} = s_j$ and, with the default penalty factors, the problem is multi_lasso(X, Y, λ / c) with $Y = [y_1 \cdots y_q]$; a dense shared design is fitted with the model of multi_lasso. Its nobs is then $qn$, where multi_lasso reports $n$.
  • Method. The tasks are centered and standardized each on its own, the rows of task $t$ are scaled by $\sqrt{N c_t / n_t}$, and the blocks are placed on the diagonal of a sparse $N \times pq$ design over $\operatorname{vec}(B)$, whose groups are the rows of $B$; group coordinate descent solves the least-squares problem on it, with coordinate orders drawn from rng. The columns of different tasks are orthogonal, so the curvature over a row is diagonal. With standardization and equal weights it is a multiple of the identity and each block step is exact; otherwise a step minimizes a majorization, and convergence takes more passes. Momentum is on by default here: each pass over the active rows starts from a point extrapolated from the last two, which saves passes at small penalties. momentum = false or order = :cyclic turns it off (see CDOptions). A dense shared design keeps the options as multi_lasso takes them, without momentum unless momentum = true.
  • Sparse designs. A sparse $X_t$ stays sparse without an intercept. With an intercept, centering fills it in: its block in the stacked design is dense, about $n_t p$ stored entries.
  • The results. A MultitaskLassoSolution holds $B$ as a sparse $p \times q$ matrix, the intercepts, the active rows, nobs ($N$), task_nobs (the $n_t$) and the task weights. coef gives the $(p + 1) \times q$ matrix with the intercepts in the first row, predict(sol, Xs_new) a vector with the predictions of each task at its own new design, and dof the number of nonzero coefficients plus $q$ with an intercept.

MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) solves along a decreasing sequence of levels, each warm started from the previous solution, with the sequential strong rule on the rows as in MultiLassoPath. By default it has nλ = 100 levels spaced evenly on the log scale from $\lambda_{\max}(1 + \sqrt{\varepsilon})$, where

\[\lambda_{\max} = \max_j \frac{1}{\omega_j}\,\Big\|\Big(\frac{c_t\,\tilde x_{tj}^\top \tilde y_t}{n_t\,s_{tj}}\Big)_{t:\,s_{tj} > 0}\Big\|_2\]

is the smallest level whose solution is $B = 0$ ($\tilde x_{tj}$ and $\tilde y_t$ are centered with an intercept), down to λminratio times $\lambda_{\max}$, with λminratio 0.01 when $N < pq$ and $10^{-4}$ otherwise: the grid of LassoPath for one task and of MultiLassoPath for a shared design. MultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys, λpath) takes levels of your own, and max_rows stops the path after the first level with more nonzero rows. coef(path) gives a matrix per level, coef(path, λ) one matrix, interpolated in $\lambda$, and predict(path, Xs_new) a vector of $q$ matrices, of size $n_t \times L$ for $L$ levels. fit(MultitaskLassoPath, Xs, ys) and fit(CVMultitaskLassoPath, Xs, ys) are the constructors.

CVMultitaskLassoPath(Xs, ys) chooses the level by $K$-fold cross-validation (nfolds = 10), with the folds drawn within each task: the observations of each task are assigned to the $K$ folds in a balanced way, as CVLassoPath assigns those of one data set, so that every fold holds out part of every task. folds takes one vector of labels $1, \dots, K$ per task instead, with every label used in every task. The path on all the data gives the levels, and each training fold refits the same procedure on its own observations: the centering, the scales, the :observations weights and the stacked design are computed again, so nothing leaks from the held-out part. Every training fold must keep at least two observations of each task with an intercept, and one without. The held-out error of fold $k$ at a level is

\[e_k = \frac{1}{w_k}\sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t}{n_t}\sum_{i \in \mathrm{test}(t, k)} (y_{ti} - \hat y_{ti})^2, \qquad w_k = \sum_{t=1}^q \frac{c_t\, m_{tk}}{n_t},\]

with $m_{tk}$ the number of observations of task $t$ in fold $k$, and $c_t$, $n_t$ those of the full data. cvm averages the $e_k$ with the weights $w_k$, and cvse, λmin and λ1se follow as for CVLassoPath. A fold path that stops early leaves the remaining levels out of the averages; nonconverged fold levels are also left out and cannot be selected, and the levels that no fold reaches have NaN in cvm, cvse and cvm_task and are not candidates for λmin and λ1se. At the levels every fold reaches, cvm is $\sum_t c_t \mathrm{MSE}_t / \sum_t c_t$, the held-out counterpart of the training loss, where $\mathrm{MSE}_t$, the held-out mean squared error of task $t$, is cvm_task[t, :]. With one task and the same folds, cvm, cvse, λmin and λ1se are those of CVLassoPath(X, y; intercept = true). nrows counts the nonzero rows of path at each level, and coef(cv) and predict(cv, Xs_new) use the solution at λ1se, or at λmin with λ = :λmin. The folds run in parallel on the available threads, each with a generator seeded from rng, and the result does not depend on the number of threads. The example on multitask regression fits three tasks of different sizes and compares the selection with separate lasso fits.

Other multitask row penalties

multitask_lasso, MultitaskLassoPath, CVMultitaskLassoPath, multi_lasso and MultiLassoPath accept row_norm = :l2 and alpha = 0 by default. They penalize one coefficient matrix. Write $U_{jt} = s_{tj} B_{jt}$, with the scales and task weights defined above. The objective is

\[\sum_t \frac{c_t}{2n_t}\|y_t-\beta_{0t}-X_tb_t\|^2 + \lambda\sum_j h_j(U_{j\cdot}), \qquad h_j(u)=\begin{cases} \omega_j\|u\|_\infty, & \texttt{row\_norm = :linf},\\ \alpha\|u\|_1+(1-\alpha)\omega_j\|u\|_2, & \texttt{row\_norm = :l2}. \end{cases}\]

alpha must lie in $[0,1]$; row_norm = :linf requires alpha = 0. The default is the row ℓ1/ℓ2 penalty. row_norm = :linf gives row ℓ1/ℓ∞, which often has several entries tied at the largest absolute value in a row. With row_norm = :l2, alpha > 0, the sparse group penalty combines row selection with entrywise sparsity. At alpha = 1 the tasks are separate lassos at $\lambda/c_t$. Row selection allows zero entries within a selected row for all three penalties; it does not force each selected predictor to have a nonzero coefficient in every task.

The factors $\omega_j$ weight the row term only. A zero factor under the sparse group leaves the entrywise term $\alpha\lambda\|u\|_1$ in place. The automatic grid needs finite $\lambda_{\max}$; unpenalized rows that have a nonzero gradient at zero require explicit levels. The multi_lasso family has no penalty factors or task weights: it uses one shared design, $c_t=1$, and loss $\|Y-1\beta_0^\top-XB\|_F^2/(2n)$. Its nobs is $n$.

Let $g_j$ be the gradient of the loss with respect to row $U_{j\cdot}$, restricted to coordinates with $s_{tj}>0$. At zero coefficients, with the intercepts profiled, the thresholds are

\[\lambda_{\max}^{(\infty)}=\max_j\frac{\|g_j(0)\|_1}{\omega_j},\qquad \lambda_{\max}^{(\mathrm{sg})}=\max_j\rho_j, \quad \|\operatorname{soft}(g_j(0),\alpha\rho_j)\|_2=(1-\alpha)\omega_j\rho_j.\]

The first maximum uses positive factors; a zero-factor row with a nonzero correlation has no finite threshold. For sparse group with $\omega_j=0$ and $\alpha>0$, $\rho_j=\|g_j(0)\|_\infty/\alpha$. The roots are computed exactly between the breakpoints of soft thresholding.

A zero row is optimal when $\|g_j\|_1\le\lambda\omega_j$ for ℓ∞, or $\|\operatorname{soft}(g_j,\alpha\lambda)\|_2\le(1-\alpha)\lambda\omega_j$ for sparse group. On a nonzero ℓ∞ row, $\|g_j\|_1=\lambda\omega_j$; the gradient is supported on entries of maximal absolute coefficient, with opposite signs. On a nonzero sparse group row,

\[0\in g_j+(1-\alpha)\lambda\omega_j\frac{U_{j\cdot}}{\|U_{j\cdot}\|_2} +\alpha\lambda\,\partial\|U_{j\cdot}\|_1.\]

Each task's residuals sum to zero when intercepts are fitted. Screening uses the sequential strong rule: a row is kept if its gradient's dual norm at the previous solution is at least $2\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-1}$. For sparse group that norm is the root above. Every excluded row is checked against its zero-row optimality condition after solving; violators are added and the level is solved again. kkt_checks records these rounds.

The row ℓ1/ℓ∞ and sparse-group penalties use row coordinate descent on the tasks' own designs. No stacked design is built, and a shared design is stored once. The solver keeps per-task residuals and centered column squared norms. A row uses the majorizing curvature $L_j=\max_{t:s_{tj}>0} c_t\|\tilde x_{tj}\|^2/(n_t s_{tj}^2)$ and a proximal step of size $1/L_j$. With standardized columns and equal weights, this step is exact. A full pass costs $O(p\sum_t n_t)$ for dense designs, plus $O(pq\log q)$ for the ℓ∞ proximal steps ($O(pq)$ for sparse group). The residual cache costs $O(\sum_t n_t)$ and column/coefficient caches $O(pq)$. Sparse designs are centered on the fly, including with intercepts, without densifying them.

Full passes alternate with passes on active rows, in cyclic order or random order from rng. A full pass confirms convergence. With relative_tol, each change $\Delta U_{jt}$ is measured in fitted-value units as $|\Delta U_{jt}|\sqrt{a_{tj}}$, where $a_{tj}=c_t\|\tilde x_{tj}\|^2/(n_t s_{tj}^2)$. The largest such change is compared with $\texttt{opt\_tol}\,r$, with response scale $r=\sqrt{\sum_t c_t\|\tilde y_t\|^2/n_t}$. Columns and responses are centered with an intercept. The scale has a positive numerical floor; when $r=0$, zero coefficients solve the problem exactly. Without relative_tol, opt_tol bounds the absolute change of $U$. A positive gaptol also requires a relative duality gap below that target. Paths default to max(ProximalSolvers.PATH_GAPTOL, 64eps(T)); single fits require the gap only with an explicit positive gaptol, except continuation with warm_start = false, which uses the path default. gaptol = 0 uses the change rule alone. The dual point scales the weighted residuals into all row dual balls. No gap is used at $\lambda=0$, with a completely unpenalized row, or when the primal objective is zero. Reaching max_iter passes gives converged = false. The default ℓ1/ℓ2 solver keeps its existing behavior.

sparse_group_lasso has one response and user-defined groups of predictors. Here a group is one predictor across tasks, with a common entrywise penalty. A dirty model decomposes $B = A + S$ with different penalties on the two components; it is not one of these penalties.

Cross-validation with a shared design

CVMultiLassoPath(X, Y) uses the same observation folds for all responses. It accepts positional CDOptions, nfolds = 10, explicit folds, rng, threads, nλ, λminratio, and the keywords of MultiLassoPath. Every training fold refits centering, scales and coefficients on its own rows at the full-data grid. If fold $k$ has $m_k$ observations, its error is $e_k=\sum_{i\in k,t}(Y_{it}-\hat Y_{it})^2/(m_kq)$. cvm averages these errors with weights $m_k$; cvse is their standard error, as for CVLassoPath. cvm_response holds the held-out MSE for each response. With one response and the same folds, fits and standardization, these summaries and selected levels agree with CVLassoPath(X, y; intercept = true, standardize_x = true).

Both multitask CV types treat a nonconverged fold level as an unreached level: its fold error is NaN, and it is never selected. Means use the available folds; λmin and λ1se compare levels that converged in every fold. coef(cv) and predict(cv, Xnew) use λ1se, or λmin with λ = :λmin. Fold generators are seeded separately from rng, so threading does not change the results. See examples/13_multitask_penalties.jl for a comparison of the three penalties on shared and task-specific supports.

The dirty multitask model

dirty_multitask fits the model of Jalali, Ravikumar, Sanghavi and Ruan (2010). It estimates a total coefficient matrix $B=A+S$ with separate penalties on its components:

\[\min_{A,S,\beta_0}\; \sum_t\frac{c_t}{2n_t}\|y_t-\beta_{0t}-X_t(a_t+s_t)\|^2 +\sum_j\omega_j\bigl(\lambda_b\|\widetilde A_{j\cdot}\|_\infty +\lambda_s\|\widetilde S_{j\cdot}\|_1\bigr).\]

The arguments λ_shared and λ_specific are the positional levels $\lambda_b$ and $\lambda_s$. The default shared_norm = :linf is the paper's shared norm; shared_norm = :l2 is a variant. Designs may be shared (dirty_multitask(X, Y, λb, λs)) or task-specific (Xs, ys). The intercepts, scales and task weights follow multitask_lasso. Write $E_j=\{t:s_{tj}>0\}$, $q_j=|E_j|$ and $v=(s_{tj}B_{jt})_{t\in E_j}$. Both components stay zero outside $E_j$. With task_weights = :equal, each task has loss $\|r_t\|^2/(2n_t)$.

Reduction and computation

The loss depends on the total $B$. Minimizing over its split gives the row penalty and its dual ball

\[h_j(v)=\omega_j\min_{a+s=v} \{\lambda_b\|a\|+\lambda_s\|s\|_1\},\qquad C_j=\omega_j\{z:\|z\|_*\le\lambda_b,\ \|z\|_\infty\le\lambda_s\},\]

where the dual norm $\|\cdot\|_*$ is ℓ1 for the shared ℓ∞ norm and ℓ2 for the shared ℓ2 norm. Equivalently, $h_j(v)=\sup_{z\in C_j}\langle z,v\rangle$. For ℓ∞, this is the sorted ℓ1 penalty

\[h_j(v)=\omega_j\sum_{i=1}^{q_j} \min\{\lambda_s,\max(\lambda_b-(i-1)\lambda_s,0)\}\,|v|_{(i)}, \qquad |v|_{(1)}\ge\cdots\ge|v|_{(q_j)}.\]

ProximalBase's SLOPE computes its value and proximal step, with buffers owned by each fit. The ℓ2 variant uses the proximal step $u-\Pi_{C_j}(u)$. Projection onto the intersection of the ℓ2 ball of radius $c$ and the ℓ∞ ball of radius $d$ has entries $z_i=\operatorname{clip}(u_i/(1+\mu),-d,d)$. Here $\mu=0$ if clipping is feasible, otherwise its positive root enforces $\|z\|_2=c$. Zero radii are handled directly. Both variants use the row solver on the tasks' designs, including sparse designs with intercepts, without a stacked design.

The penalty factors multiply the whole row penalty. Thus $\omega_j=0$ leaves the row unpenalized. In the multitask sparse group above, a zero factor leaves its entrywise penalty in place. The dirty model also differs from sparse_group_lasso, which has one response and groups of predictors, and from multitask sparse group, which applies both penalties to a single $B$. With one task, applying levels $a,b$ to the same coefficient gives $(a+b)|B|$; the dirty model gives $\min(a,b)|B|$.

Regimes and the returned components

For positive levels, let $r=\lambda_b/\lambda_s$. On each row's fitted coordinates the ℓ∞ estimator reduces to row ℓ1/ℓ∞ when $r\le1$ and to the entrywise lasso when $r\ge q_j$. For ℓ2 the corresponding regimes are the group lasso when $r\le1$ and the entrywise lasso when $r\ge\sqrt{q_j}$. Factors do not change these regimes. Interior ratios allow both components; they need not both be nonzero.

The returned split is computed in standardized coordinates and then divided by the scales. It follows these conventions, in order:

  • If $\lambda_b=0$, return $A=B$. If only $\lambda_s=0$, return $S=B$. Both zero gives $A=B$. A zero level makes the row penalty zero.
  • For ℓ∞, return $A=0$ when $r\ge q_j$. For ℓ2, return $A=0$ when $r\ge\sqrt{m_v}$, where $m_v$ counts the nonzero entries of $v$.
  • Otherwise, return $S=0$ when $r\le1$.
  • In the interior, return $a_i=\operatorname{clip}(v_i,-\tau,\tau)$ and $s=v-a$. For ℓ∞, $\tau=|v|_{(\lceil r\rceil)}$. At an integer $r=k$, every threshold in $[|v|_{(k+1)},|v|_{(k)}]$ minimizes the row penalty; this rule takes $|v|_{(k)}$, the most shared choice. For ℓ2, $\tau$ is the unique positive root of $\sum_i\min(|v_i|/\tau,1)^2=r^2$. Sorted intervals give $\tau^2=\sum_{i>k}|v|_{(i)}^2/(r^2-k)$ on $[|v|_{(k+1)},|v|_{(k)}]$ (upper endpoint infinite for $k=0$).

An estimator's reduction, the existence of a pure optimal split, and the split returned by this convention are different statements. For example, the ℓ2 rule can return a pure specific split for a particular row before the global lasso regime. Components need not be unique even when total coefficients are unique. sharedrows counts the nonzero rows of this canonical $A$; it does not identify shared features or a latent decomposition from the data.

Paths, tuning and diagnostics

DirtyMultitaskPath(Xs, ys; ratio = r) holds $r$ fixed and varies $\lambda=\lambda_s$, with $\lambda_b=r\lambda$. Its automatic grid starts at $\lambda_{\max}(1+\sqrt{\mathrm{eps}})$, where

\[d_j(g)=\frac{\max\{\|g\|_*/r,\|g\|_\infty\}}{\omega_j},\qquad \lambda_{\max}(r)=\max_j d_j(g_j(0)).\]

The gradient is on the fitted standardized coordinates, with intercepts profiled. An unpenalized row with a nonzero gradient has infinite threshold and needs explicit levels. Screening compares $d_j$ with $2\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-1}$, then checks every excluded row and repairs any violations. Total coefficients warm start the next level; components are split anew. coef(path, λ) requires a fitted level.

CVDirtyMultitaskPath compares ratios and levels by the held-out score of CVMultitaskLassoPath. Defaults are five geometric ratios on $[1,q]$ for ℓ∞ or $[1,\sqrt q]$ for ℓ2. Per-task folds are common across ratios; with a shared design, whole observation rows are held out across responses. Each training fit refits centering, scales and task weights. cvm is a nratios × nλ matrix, with NaN padding for shorter paths. Selection requires convergence on the full data and every fold, minimizes cvm, and breaks ties by larger ratio, then larger specific level. There is no two-dimensional one-standard-error rule. coef(cv) and predict(cv, Xnew) use the selected full-data fit.

A fit's kkt recomputes the two-component conditions at the returned point. Writing $z_j=-\nabla_j f(B)$, these are membership in $C_j$ and

\[\langle z_j,\widetilde A_{j\cdot}\rangle =\omega_j\lambda_b\|\widetilde A_{j\cdot}\|,\qquad \langle z_j,\widetilde S_{j\cdot}\rangle =\omega_j\lambda_s\|\widetilde S_{j\cdot}\|_1,\]

together with intercept stationarity, zero dropped coordinates and $B=A+S$. kkt is the largest violation divided by the maximum of one and the magnitudes of the terms being compared. gap is the relative duality gap, using residual scaling $\min(1,\lambda/\max_j d_j)$; it is NaN when unavailable, including zero levels, unpenalized rows and zero objective. Convergence requires the row solver's change rule and any requested available gap target. nobs is $\sum_t n_t$. dof counts nonzero entries in total $B$ plus fitted intercepts; it is not effective degrees of freedom. See examples/15_multitask_dirty.jl for a small comparison with row penalties and separate lasso fits on shared and task-specific supports.
